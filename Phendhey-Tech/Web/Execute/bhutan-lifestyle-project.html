Respondent Distribution

366 responses were collected across urban, rural, and semi-urban areas of Bhutan. After excluding one non-resident, 358 responses were retained for analysis. The majority of respondents were urban participants, reflecting Bhutan's growing urbanisation trend.

02

Core Lifestyle Differences

Urban residents work slightly longer hours on average and engage primarily in mental or knowledge-based work. Rural residents report marginally higher screen use before sleep and slightly longer sleep duration — though the difference in sleep length is minimal, suggesting sleep duration alone is not the primary differentiator between groups.

03

Correlation Insights

Screen use before sleep had a consistently negative effect on both sleep quality and daytime energy in urban and rural groups alike — more pronounced among rural residents. Longer work hours had a neutral effect on urban residents but a mild adverse effect in rural populations. Longer sleep duration showed a neutral effect in urban areas and only a weakly positive effect in rural areas.

04

Habits Believed to Improve Sleep

When asked which habits contribute most to better sleep quality, respondents identified four key behaviours: reduced screen use before bed, creating a stress-free environment, maintaining a consistent sleep time, and engaging in relaxing activities before sleep. These directly inform the priority features of a sleep-support application.

05

Preferred App Features

Respondents ranked the most valued features in a sleep-support app as: reminders to reduce screen use, sleep tracking, tips for a better bedtime routine, and help managing daily schedules. The preference for phone-based, low-barrier solutions over complex wearables was clear throughout the data.

06

Market Willingness

A majority of respondents said they would be willing to use a sleep-support application. 58.42% said yes outright, 36.6% said maybe — indicating they are not opposed, only undecided — and just 4.96% said no. Combined, over 95% of respondents are open to some form of digital sleep support, representing a strong commercial opportunity.

07

Key Takeaways

Urban–rural lifestyle differences in Bhutan are real and measurable. Screen use before sleep is the most consistently harmful habit across both groups. Work patterns matter more in rural areas, while sleep duration alone is not a reliable indicator of wellbeing in urban populations. Respondents clearly prefer simple, phone-based solutions. With over 95% open to a wellness app and screen use reduction identified as the top desired feature, the case for a practical, affordable digital sleep tool in Bhutan is strong — awareness and access remain the key barriers to adoption.

How to read the "maybe" group

The 36.6% who said "maybe" are arguably the most interesting segment. They're not skeptics — they're undecided. That usually comes down to three things: not knowing enough about what the tool does, uncertainty about cost, or simply never having thought about sleep as something worth tracking.

That's not resistance. That's an awareness and education gap — and those are very solvable.

What tool do people actually want?

Out of all the options presented, respondents pointed most clearly toward one thing: a sleep tracking app.

That's a telling choice. A sleep tracking app lives on a phone most people already own — no extra device to buy, no subscription to commit to, no steep learning curve. It's the lowest-barrier, highest-convenience option available. Respondents aren't asking for something complicated or expensive. They're asking for something simple that fits into the life they already have.

💡
Preferred tool: Sleep tracking app — chosen for convenience, low cost, and ease of use on existing smartphones.

The honest picture

There are real challenges worth acknowledging. Smartphone and internet access is still uneven across Bhutan, particularly in rural areas. Awareness of sleep health as something worth actively managing is still developing. And affordability will matter — any tool priced out of reach simply won't get used.

But none of these are dealbreakers. They're design constraints — they tell developers and policymakers how to build and distribute these tools, not whether to.

Bottom line

The data speaks clearly. With 58.42% ready to say yes and another 36.6% saying maybe, Bhutan's wellness market is early-stage but unmistakably real. People know what they want — something simple, on their phone, that helps them sleep better. The demand is there. It just needs the right product to meet it.
